# Judge rejects LaSalle County suit against TxDOT on shale oil road fund
A 2013 Texas law that legislators crafted to direct $225 million to county roads hit hardest by the oil and gas shale boom has been interpreted such that the money would instead go to 75 percent of the state’s counties.
And as of last week, 13 months after Gov. Rick Perry signed Senate Bill 1747 into law, none of that money had yet made its way into county coffers, a Texas Department of Transportation official said in a court hearing Thursday.

LaSalle County, home to some of the first wells drilled in the Eagle Ford Shale boom in South Texas, Thursday was foiled in an attempt to make TxDOT be more selective in passing out that money. A Travis County district judge, in a lawsuit LaSalle County filed against TxDOT, ruled Thursday that TxDOT is following the law and thus the county doesn’t have standing to sue the agency.
The county claimed the agency misinterpreted the Legislature’s intent when it created the County Transportation Infrastructure Fund by allocating $41 million of that $225 million to 81 counties that had declining oil and gas production or no active oil and gas wells at all.
Instead, legislators meant for that money to go to counties that saw notable increases in oil and gas production last year, LaSalle County argued.
However, TxDOT accepted into the program all 191 counties that applied, even some whose initial applications were deemed “deficient,” TxDOT engineering operations director Mark Marek testified Thursday. The grants would range from $118,566 for Presidio County in West Texas to $9,419,502 to Andrews County, north of Odessa.
Article continues below this ad
“It starts to spread out to amounts that don’t do much good for the roads affected” by the boom, said Don Cruse, a lawyer for LaSalle County, at Thursday’s hearing before Travis County District Judge Stephen Yelenosky. The county asked that the court issue an order temporarily preventing TxDOT from handing out the grant money.
TxDOT officials said they are only administering the law as written, using a formula that takes into account not only oil and gas production, but also overweight trucking permits granted in each county. The agency asked that the court throw out the lawsuit, arguing that it is protected from the suit by the concept of sovereign immunity.
Yelenosky agreed, in effect ending the lawsuit.
The 2013 law set out several requirements for counties to qualify for the grant money, including the creation in each interested county of a special district where increased property taxes would be captured and be available for road repairs. The grants are contingent on each county putting up a matching 20 percent of what TxDOT gives them.

But LaSalle County lawyers in court Thursday pointed out that in many cases, the districts included only public road right of way, land that isn’t subject to property taxation and thus cannot produce incremental tax revenue.
Marek also acknowledged that in the case of a second requirement — that the counties submit road condition reports as part of their application for the grants — TxDOT merely checked to see if such a report was submitted and didn’t analyze the reports.
“We’ve got a problem,” LaSalle County Judge Joel Rodriguez Jr. told the American-Statesman in an interview outside the hearing room. “You’re going to have to have legislation to clean this up.”
