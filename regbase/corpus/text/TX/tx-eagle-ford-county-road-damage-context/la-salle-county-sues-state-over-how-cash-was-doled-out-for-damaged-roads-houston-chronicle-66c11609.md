# La Salle County sues state over how cash was doled out for damaged roads
La Salle County, whose location over the Eagle Ford Shale play has led to a booming economy but also degradation of area roads, has sued the Texas Department of Transportation over how the agency has doled out grant money for roads damaged by the energy industry across the state.
After the Legislature last year authorized $225 million in grants to repair the crumbling roads, counties were told to follow a lengthy application process as part of the creation of TxDOT's County Transportation Infrastructure Fund. Officials were required to produce road condition reports, create special taxing districts and provide a 20 percent funding match so TxDOT could determine how to divvy up the money.
But County Judge Joel Rodriguez Jr. said he believes that few counties followed the process or the statutory regulations.
"It's been treated like a block grant," Rodriguez said. "Everybody qualifies."

The lawsuit is asking the court to determine whether TxDOT did set requirements that other counties had to follow in order to qualify for the grants. If so, the plaintiffs want to ensure that no grant goes to a county that did not follow the rules. Rodriguez said he put more than 500 hours into researching whether other counties filed the appropriate documents for the application process.
$6.5 million for La Salle
"We think TxDOT did not follow their own rules and regulations and what was intended at the Legislature," said Donato D. Ramos, an attorney based in Laredo who is representing the county. "We're asking the court to make that determination."
In response to questions, TxDOT spokeswoman Becky Ozuna said the agency is unable to comment on pending litigation.

Of the state's 254 counties, TxDOT awarded grants to 191, including several on the Eagle Ford Shale: Karnes, Live Oak, DeWitt, Dimmit and Goliad. La Salle received about $6.5 million. Rodriguez said the needs are closer to $400 million.
"We were a little surprised, perhaps, at the amount that was allocated to La Salle County, since the whole purpose and intent of this grant was to assist those counties that are most impacted by this energy activity," Ramos said.
Ozuna said the money was divvied up by formula, as governed by Senate Bill 1747, the legislation that created the grant program.
Searching for answers
According to the lawsuit, La Salle County ranks third in the state in oil and gas production volume. The county more than 2,000 production wells, which could result in 20 billion vehicle trips per year to drill, produce and operate those wells, according to the lawsuit.

Ramos said La Salle officials contacted TxDOT about why some counties received funding but never got an answer. In an email, Ozuna said: "All counties, including La Salle, were assisted in the process if they reached out to TxDOT and asked for help."
Ramos said he has not contacted or heard from other counties but that he believes "that every county in the state should be concerned whether or not a state agency is appropriately following its own rules and mandates with regards to the appropriation of grant funds."
DeWitt County Judge Daryl Fowler agreed that the nearly $5 million that TxDOT awarded his county isn't enough to fix its road damage, the cost of which, as in La Salle, is believed to be close to $400 million.
But he said the Legislature had to allow for flexibility in the grant program if it was going to get enough statewide support to pass the law. Fowler hopes the initial grants will lead to "something bigger and better down the road." He plans to fight for more money to fix county roads in the state's energy regions during the next legislative session.

"I am of the opinion that these battles are better fought in the Legislature," he said.
