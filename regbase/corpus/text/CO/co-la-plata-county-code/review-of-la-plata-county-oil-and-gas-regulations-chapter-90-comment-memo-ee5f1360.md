REVIEW OF LA PLATA COUNTY OIL AND GAS REGULATIONS (CHAPTER 90) 
October 7, 2022 

The following comments are submitted by the San Juan Citizens Alliance. The Alliance is an 
established Four Corners conservation organization with deep roots in the issue of coal bed 
methane development in La Plata County. Since the 1980’s our members have been involved in 
every rulemaking the County has undertaken. This historic involvement comes from the belief that 
the oil and gas industry must be held accountable at the local level for its impacts to our land, air, 
water, and people. That is the principle that guided us in the 1980’s and guides us today. 

The Alliance appreciates the good work that has occurred on this draft. There are many issues 
where La Plata County has proposed changes that either meet or exceed state standards. There are 
other issues where we do not believe the county has gone far enough to protect public health, safety, 
welfare, and the environment. 

The Alliance is requesting substantial revisions in the following areas: Setbacks from homes and 
schools, pipeline setbacks, air quality protections, and financial assurances. 

We have outlined some of these changes below and can send redlines to the entire draft document if 
that would be helpful. 

Sec. 90-2 - Jurisdiction; authority and applicability. 

1). Sec. 90-2. I: Legal authority for County oil and gas regulations does not list local government land 
use authority. Granting local governments’ land use authority over oil and gas development was one 
of the most important and significant changes brought about by Senate Bill 19-181. 

On April 16, 2019, Governor Polis signed into law S.B.19-181, also known as "Protect Public Welfare 
Oil and Gas Operations," which prioritizes the protection of public s afety, health, welfare, and the 
environment in regulation of the oil and gas industry . S .B.19-181 modifies the Oil and Gas 
Conservation Act, C.R.S. § 34-60-101, et seq., the Local Government Land Use Control Enabling Act, 
C.R.S. § 29 -20-101, et seq ., and o ther relevant statutes, and grants additional authority to local 
governments to regulate oil and gas development . S.B.19-181 specifically clarifie s that local 
governments have land use authority to minimize adverse impacts to public safety, health, welfare, 
and the environment and to regulate land use and surface impacts, including the ability to: regulate 
the siting of oil and gas locations; regulate noise, odor, and vibration from oil and gas facilities; inspect 
oil and gas facilities; impose fines for leaks, spills, and emissions; and impose fees on operators or 
owners to cover the reasonably foreseeable, direct and indirect costs of permitting and regulating, 
monitoring and inspection programs, and enforcing local governmental requirements.

Having been involved in the COGCC Mission Change rulemaking, the La Plata County staff 
understands the origin of the COGCC’s 2,000-foot setback from residential building units. From the 
Mission Change Rulemaking’s Statement of Basis and Purpose: 

The Commission’s choice of 2,000 feet is a conclusion based on the Commission’s review of 
all the evidence in the administrative record, and on weighing the relative merits and 
critiques of each peer-reviewed scientific study. A core justification, though not the only 
justification, for the Commission’s choice of 2,000 feet is the Final Report: Human Health Risk 
Assessment for Oil and Gas Operations in Colorado, published by CDPHE on October 17, 2019. 
The study used monitoring and modeling techniques to identify pollutant concentrations 
above acute health risk thresholds at distances of 2,000 feet from oil and gas facilities under 
some scenarios. The CDPHE study supports the Commission’s cautious approach to 
protecting people’s health and provides evidence that the Commission’s choice of a 2,000-
foot setback is appropriate. The Commission recognizes that, like all scientific studies, the 
CDPHE study has both advantages and disadvantages to its chosen methodologies. It 
provides one of the most robust and Colorado-specific sources of data about health impacts 
that are likely attributable to proximity to oil and gas development. However, like all 
scientific studies that employ monitoring techniques, it is limited by the quality and 
representativeness of the data gathered, and like all scientific studies that employ modeling 
techniques, it is limited by the uncertainty and assumptions inherent in the model. 

For these reasons, the Commission determined that it was not appropriate to rely solely on 
the CDPHE study as the basis for its decision to adopt a 2,000-foot setback in Rule 604. 
However, when considering the relative merits of the CDPHE study in conjunction with all 
evidence in the administrative record, the Commission determined that the CDHPE study is 
one appropriate study to rely on as a basis for its decision to adopt a 2,000 foot setback in 
Rules 604.a.(3) and 604.b. The Commission also found it compelling that the short-term 
health impacts anticipated by the CDPHE study mirror complaints the Commission received 
in recent years from people living in proximity to oil and gas locations. 

Although protecting public health was one reason that the Commission chose to adopt a 
2,000-foot distance in Rules 604.a.(3) and 604.b, it was not the only reason. The Commission 
also chose the 2,000-foot setback because of its statutory mandate to protect and minimize 
adverse impacts to public welfare and public safety. C.R.S. § 34-60-106(2.5)(a). Each of the 
reasons discussed below is a standalone reason for the Commission’s decision to adopt a 
2,000-foot setback in Rules 604.a.(3) and 604.b. 

First, as discussed above in Rule 423, the Commission Staff’s review of the applicable 
literature demonstrated that most noise from oil and gas operations dissipates to a point that 
it is no longer disruptive to human receptors at 2,000 feet. Second, oil and gas operations

result in substantial amounts of truck traffic. While the Commission has authority to regulate 
traffic on access roads, it has limited authority to regulate most aspects of traffic from oil 
and gas development. The Commission determined that a 2,000-foot distance is an

Finally, oil and gas operations also result in a range of other adverse impacts to public 
welfare, including light, dust, and odors. The Commission determined that a 2,000-foot

each of these categories of impacts. 

For all these reasons, the Commission determined a 2,000-foot distance met the standard set

and Purpose but would like to add a few points specific to the risk of hazardous air pollution, health 
complaints, and risk of fires and explosions. 

Risk of Hazardous Air Pollution 

The 2,000-foot setback stems from the 2019 “Final Report: Human Health Risk Assessment for Oil 
& Gas Operations in Colorado”3 (“Health Risk Assessment”). Commissioned by the Colorado 
Department of Public Health and Environment, the Health Risk Assessment analyzed canister 
samples taken from around oil and gas facilities in both Weld and Garfield counties. The data 
collected from the sampling was modeled and the data showed that emissions from oil and gas 
development is not likely to pose a subchronic (multi-day) or chronic (long term) health risks. 
However, the study concluded that “At the most-exposed (downwind) locations 500 ft from the well 
pads, the highest estimated 1-hour exposures exceeded guideline levels for a small number of chemicals, 
including benzene, during development and production activities, and toluene and ethyltoluenes 
during development activities. One-hour exposures decreased rapidly with distance from the 
hypothetical facilities, but some remained above guideline levels out to 2,000 ft.”4 Acute exposures 
to these chemicals “can be associated with headaches, nosebleeds, fatigue, dizziness, etc., depending 
on the chemical, intensity of exposure, and sensitivity of the individual.”5

have found evidence of birth defects and pre-term birth among mothers who live within 2640 feet

Risk of Fires and Explosions 

Air pollution is not the only risk to public health and safety. The risk of fires and explosions also 
must be considered. Well fires and explosions in Colorado are discussed in Benjamin Blair’s “Is 
reporting “significant damage” transparent? Assessing fire and explosion risk at oil and gas 
operations in the United States” (2017).8 The study found that between 2006 and 2015, a total of 
116 fires and explosions were self-reported by the industry in Colorado (0.03% of active wells). The 
study concludes that the total number of fires and explosions may be underreported due to 
Colorado’s relaxed incident reporting rules. 

The damage these fires and explosions cause is based on the size and intesity of the fire and 
explosion, and the proximity to homes. Multi-well pads increase the size and complexity of 
operations, increasing the potential risk for those living and working near these sites.9 

In summary, SJCA believes there is good justification to require a greater minimum setback than the 
500-foot floor proposed by La Plata County. We believe that the County should mirror the 
expectation for a 2,000-foot setback with a 500 minimum that may only be reduced by a waiver 
from all affected building unit owners and tenants. 

Adopting the COGCC’s 2,000-foot setback standard would allow the County to determine for itself 
what are the “substantially equivalent protections” necessary to protect public health, safety, welfare,

Methods of assessing risk and appropriate setbacks for pipelines are well established. In the case of 
natural gas lines, these distances or setbacks can be determined by application of the industry-

ignited rupture of a high-pressure natural gas pipeline.” The diameter and operating pressure of a 
pipeline are put into the equation and the size of the affected area, in the event of a credible worst-
case failure event, is determined. (Exhibit 2.)

PIPA report looks at the various risks with oil pipelines such as the risks to crops, water quality, as 
well as the risks of fires and explosions, toxicity and asphyxiation effects from vapor clouds that can 
be associated with oil pipeline ruptures. (Exhibit 3.)
