PREHEARING STATEMENT OF THE CITY AND COUNTY OF BROOMFIELD 

I. Introduction 

The City and County of Broomfield (“CCOB”), by and through their undersigned attorneys, 
respectfully submit this Prehearing Statement in the above-captioned rulemaking (“rulemaking”). 

The CCOB is concerned about the risks that accidents and leaks involving flowline and crude oil 
transfer lines (cumulatively “pipelines”) pose to human health and the environment. As 
indicated by recent accidents in Colorado and elsewhere, pipelines have the potential to rupture 
and to leak.1 Both types of integrity failures can cause significant harm to individuals, property, 
and the environment. Fortunately, proper construction, maintenance, integrity maintenance 
including leak inspections and repairs, and proper abandonment, can reduce these risks. 
Accordingly, we are pleased that the Colorado Oil and Gas Conservation Commission 
(“OGCC”) has proposed to update its pipeline requirements. While the draft represents a good 
first step toward reducing the risks posed by pipelines, more must be done. We offer suggestions 
below regarding improvements to the corrosion control requirements, integrity management 
requirements, bonding and insurance requirements, and notice and reporting provisions. These 
suggestions are intended to provide an additional layer of redundancy and therefore 
protectiveness to the proposed corrosion control, integrity management provisions, ensure that 
operators have the financial resources to adequately respond to, remediate, and compensate the 
victims of pipeline leaks or accidents, and provide local governments access to information 
necessary to perform critical oversight duties. 

The CCOB requests 1 hour to present evidence and argument at the rulemaking hearing. 

II. All Crude Oil Transfer Lines and Oil and Produced Water Flowlines Should Be 
Subject to the Federal Corrosion Control Requirements 

Corrosion threatens the integrity and safety of pipelines carrying crude oil or produced water. 
To help prevention corrosion, we recommend operators comply with federal corrosion and 
control prevention programs as detailed in our alternative proposal.

We urge the OGCC to strengthen the integrity management requirements to more closely mirror 
federal pipeline safety requirements by requiring redundant forms of leak monitoring and 
tailoring requirements to the risks posed by crude oil transfer lines and above-ground and buried 
flowlines other than dump lines and small diameter peripheral piping. 

The current draft relies primarily on the use of pressure tests to monitor the integrity of buried 
pipelines and visual inspections to monitor the integrity of above-ground lines. Operators have 
the option to conduct an annual or a triennial pressure test, or utilize continuous pressure 
monitoring, for belowground flowlines.2 Operators need only conduct visual inspections, which 
can include sensory-based audio, visual, or olfactory detections, on aboveground flowlines.3 
Operators of crude oil transfer lines have the option of conducting annual pressure tests, 
continuous pressure monitoring, or triennial smart pigging.4 Continuous leak monitoring is only 
required for crude oil transfer lines.5 

By contrast, federal rules applicable to natural gas and hazardous liquid pipelines (e.g., crude oil 
pipelines) rely on a suite of approaches to minimize leaks. These approaches include leakage 
surveys with leak detection equipment, pressure tests or other forms of integrity testing, and 
integrity management programs that rely on a comprehensive risk analysis to identify threats to, 
and prevent releases from, pipelines.6 The specific requirements applicable to a particular 
pipeline segment differ depending on pipeline characteristics (e.g., size, maximum anticipated 
operating pressure, materials) and location. The most robust requirements apply to pipelines that 
pose the greatest threats to human life, property, or in the case of hazardous liquid pipelines, 
sensitive environmental areas.7 Accordingly, an adequate program to minimize the risk of a 
pipeline accident must include redundant forms of leak monitoring and differs depending on the 
risks posed by a particular segment of pipe.8 

For these reasons, we urge OGCC to strengthen the proposed flowline integrity management 
provisions to require the following for all flowlines, except dump lines and small diameter 
peripheral piping: 

1) Continuous pressure monitoring for all new pipelines and existing pipelines located in 
Sensitive Areas, Surface Water Supply Areas and Designated Setback Areas;

(3) Annual pressure testing and leak monitoring for existing pipelines located outside 
Sensitive Areas, Surface Water Supply Areas and Designated Setback Areas. In

We also urge the COGCC to require mandatory smart-pigging triennially for all crude oil 
transfer lines, to require that underground dump lines are annually tested to all least 100 psi, and 
to define a small diameter peripheral pipe as one with a 1 inch diameter or less. 

We support the use of continuous pressure monitoring yet urge the OGCC to require this for all 
new pipelines and existing pipelines located in populated areas or near sensitive environmental 
resources where the consequences of a pipeline leak or rupture are greatest. Continuous pressure 
monitoring is superior to periodic pressure tests as it provides operators with ongoing 
information (i.e., 24 hours, 7 days a week) about the integrity of their lines.9 Furthermore, 
continuous pressure monitors must ensure that systems be “capable of being shut-in for repairs 
immediately upon discovery of an anomaly, either through automation or through a documented 
manual process.10 Prompt discovery of leaks or ruptures can significantly reduce the damage 
associated with such events, thereby minimizing harm to workers, the public and sensitive 
environmental resources.11 

We recommend a risk-based approach for existing pipelines not located near persons, property or 
critical environmental resources, wherein operators must evaluate the risk factors of particular 
pipeline segments, and determine the appropriate leak monitoring requirements accordingly. 
Risk assessment must evaluate the age, design, construction material, and leak history of the 
pipeline, at a minimum. This approach is consistent with current OGCC guidance12 and federal 
pipeline safety rules. The Director and local governments must review and agree to the risk 
assessment. 

In addition to pressure tests, we recommend all operators conduct leak monitoring of a parameter 
other than pressure using leak detection equipment. Specifically, we request operators 
continuously monitor all flow lines carrying crude oil, condensate or produced water. Operators 
of gas flowlines must inspect lines periodically with gas detection equipment. Continuous or 
frequent leak inspections, in conjunction with pressure monitoring, are necessary to provide 
adequate redundancy and robustness.13 Annual pressure tests only measure integrity at a snap-
shot in time (e.g., a pipe segment may pass a pressure test in January, yet still develop a leak in 
August, that, absent more frequent leak inspections would go undetected until the subsequent

IV. Critical Notifications and Reports Must be Submitted to Local Governments 

Local governments play a key role in overseeing activities within their jurisdiction (e.g., 
permitting and inspections) and in providing emergency response. In order for local governments 
to perform these important duties, they require up-to-date information related to critical 
activities. For these reasons, we request OGCC add the following provisions to the rule: 

a. Advanced notification to local governments of any planned activities that could 
result in releases and/or contain information related to pipeline integrity. 
Specifically: 
i. pipeline repairs; 
ii. pressure tests; 
iii. leak inspections; and 
iv. routine pipeline maintenance that involves construction equipment or 
venting. 

b. Submission of notices or records related to pipeline integrity. Specifically: 
i. reportable safety events as defined in R. 602.c. involving pipelines; 
ii. leaks identified during inspections; 
iii. repair or remediation activities taken in response to a pipeline leak, spill or 
release; 
iv. failed pressure tests. 

V. Operators Must Carry Sufficient Financial Assurances to Remediate and Respond to 
Pipeline Accidents 

We support the proposal in R. 711 to add operators of produced water transfer systems to the list 
of operators that must provide statewide blanket financial assistance in the amount of $50,000 to 
ensure compliance with the E&P waste management rules.17 Operators of crude oil transfer lines 
and flowlines should also be required to post bonds in conformance with R. 711.

Bonding alone, however, is insufficient to ensure operators have the financial resources available 
to adequately respond to and remediate pipeline accidents that cause spills, releases or ruptures. 
While OGCC rules require operators to maintain general liability insurance in the amount of one 
million dollars, this requirement only covers property damage and bodily injury to third parties, 
and is insufficient to cover the potential losses to property and third parties that a major accident 
such as a pipeline rupture may cause.18 In addition, it does not cover environmental damage, 
such as contamination of a water body, that may be caused by gradual pollution events such as 
leaks, as well as sudden and accidental pollution events. 

We urge OGCC to require operators to maintain environmental liability insuranc e with limits of 
not less than $5,000,000 for each and every occurrence and to maintain general liability with limits 
of not less than $ 5,000,000 for each and every occurrence. Such limits are consistent with the 
recent agreement reached by CCOB and Extraction Oil & Gas.19
