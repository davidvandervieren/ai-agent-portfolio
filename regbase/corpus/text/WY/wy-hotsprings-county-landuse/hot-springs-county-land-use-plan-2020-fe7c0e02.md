INTRODUCTION 

This Land Use Plan is a concise document combining the following elements: the values and 
community identity unique to Hot Springs County, specific goals and policies identified to 
protect those values and that identity, and land use project review processes and regulations 
adopted to implement those policies. The result is a single document where heritage, goals 
and policies, and regulations are intertwined. 

Wyoming State Statute §18-5-201 authorizes the County Commissioners of respective counties 
to promulgate such rules and regulations as may be necessary to promote public health, safety, 
morals and the general welfare of the county. As such, the County Commissioners may 
regulate and restrict the location and use of buildings and structures and the use, condition of 
use or occupancy of lands for residence, recreation, agriculture, industry, commerce, public 
and other purposes in the unincorporated area of the county. To that purpose, the Hot Springs 
County Land Use Planning Commission has been established to monitor compliance with 
Wyoming State Statutes and the regulations provided herein. 

GOALS 

Agriculture is basic to the Hot Springs County way of life, the economy, and the community’s 
future well-being. Agricultural resources and values should be protected and, if possible, the 
agricultural economy should be allowed to expand. This goal is served by the following goals: 

A. New developments shall be encouraged to locate near existing towns, communities or 
other areas that will minimize conflicts with agricultural practices. 

B. New residential development should be located on uplands, rather than river terraces 
suitable for irrigated agriculture. 

C. It is not intended that all areas of the County be equally accessible. Therefore, existing 
roads in major agricultural areas of the County should be upgraded only to the extent 
necessary to serve agricultural, industrial, commercial or recreational purposes in a 
particular area. 

D. To protect agriculture and expand irrigated lands, transfers of water rights from 
agriculture to other uses should be discouraged and new development should not 
disrupt existing irrigation systems.

CHAPTER ONE 

GENERAL PROVISIONS 

1-1 Background 

A. Geographical Area: Hot Springs County is located in the southern portion of the Big 
Horn Basin, in the northwestern quarter of Wyoming. It varies from semi-arid 
mountains and hills with some badlands, in the east; to a narrow, productive river valley 
in the central portion. Mountains with good grass cover, which are cut by occasional 
streams, characterize the southern and western portions of the County; the northern 
border is hilly, with badlands and sparse grass areas. 

B. Economic Data: The three principal sources of income within the county are mineral 
production, tourism and agriculture. The relative percentages that each of these 
industries contribute to the tax base varies considerably with the cost and production of 
petroleum and petroleum related activities on a year-to-year basis. Hot Springs County 
is approximately 2,022 square miles or 1,294,080 acres. Of this area only 404,619 acres 
– 31% of the land is taxable. Depending on mineral production, the importance of 
agriculture and tourism becomes more or less important with regard to employment 
and tax payments. 

C. History and Culture: Hot Springs County contains extensive archaeological evidence of 
prehistoric occupation by semi-nomadic native cultures. These are primarily in the form 
of durable artifacts such as rock art, tepee rings, and arrowheads and other stone 
implements. These artifacts, and the ancient cultures they represent, have become 
interwoven with the present-day culture of Hot Springs County. 

The first non-natives in the area were explorers and fur trappers. The Verendrye 
Brothers may have reached the County in the 1750’s. John Colter probably crossed the 
western end of the County in 1806. Trappers under the leadership of Jedidiah Smith 
were here in 1821, followed by Captain Bonneville in 1832. In 1864 Jim Bridger laid out 
the Bridger Trail which traversed from the southeastern end of the county to its north-
central border. 

Settlement began with the construction of the first cabin in the Big Horn Basin by John 
D. Woodruff in 1871, on upper Owl Creek. By 1884 most of the lands were being used 
for ranching purposes. Homesteads were taken up along the creeks, and by 1897 most 
of the suitable land was already homesteaded. In 1906 the north half of the Wind River 
Indian Reservation was opened to homesteading. Those lands were taken up by 1910. 
The original settlement of Thermopolis was located at the mouth of Owl Creek, on the 
east bank of the Big Horn River.

6 
Petroleum production began in 1910, with a wildcat well at Hamilton Dome. It was 
soon followed by discoveries at Warm Springs, Grass Creek, Golden Eagle, Gebo Dome 
and Black Mountain. 

Coal was discovered around the turn of the century and by 1906, Sam Gebo was 
developing the mines west of the Big Horn River in the northern portion of the County. 
With the arrival of the railroad to Kirby in 1908, mining began in earnest. By 1920 the 
community of Gebo had over 2000 residents, surpassing Thermopolis in population. 
The town of Crosby followed, and mining proceeded at a good pace until 1933. With 
the advent of diesel-powered locomotives on the railroads, after World War II, the 
mines at Gebo closed. 

Big Spring, located in Hot Springs State Park, is one of the largest mineral hot springs in 
the world, although flows today are approximately 30 percent of what they were 
twenty years ago. The Park was established by an Act of Congress and the State of 
Wyoming in 1896-97. With the opening of the springs to the public, the tiny 
community of Thermopolis was moved from the Big Horn River crossing of the Bridger 
Trail, at the mouth of Owl Creek, five miles south to its present location. 

1-2 Authority: This Land Use Plan is directed by W.S.§9-8-301, W.S.§18-5-201, 
§18-5-305, and §34-22-105, as well as a Resolution of the Board of County 
Commissioners of Hot Springs County, Wyoming. This Plan constitutes the Hot Springs County 
land use plan, comprehensive plan, rules and regulation for real estate subdivisions, a 
permitting system, and a Land Classification System. 

1-3 Purpose: This Land Use Plan is promulgated to promote the public health, safety, 
morals and general welfare of the residents of Hot Springs County. Hot Springs County 
recognizes and supports private property and the right of its owners to its use, enjoyment, and 
financial benefits. The primary purpose of this Land Use Plan is to: 
• Encourage the preservation of the county’s cultural heritage; 
• Minimize the threat from development to the health, safety, and welfare of 
those residing in rural areas; 
• Enforce specific standards set by the County and State for subdivision 
development. 
1-4 Relationship to other laws: In cases where State or Federal law/regulations impose 
additional or duplicate standards regarding issues addressed by this Land Use Plan, the more 
restrictive standard shall be applied, unless such regulations are determined by the Board of 
Commissioners to adversely affect the tax base, economy employment or custom and culture of 
Hot Springs County.

CHAPTER TWO 

DEFINITIONS 

2-1 Absolute policy: A policy with which a particular development must fully comply in 
order for that development to receive approval. Absolute policies require or prohibit certain 
kinds of performance by the applicant/developer. 

2-2 Accessory use: A subordinate use, clearly incidental to the primary purpose of a 
structure, building or use of land although located on the same lot as that of the principle 
structure, building or use. 

2-3 Bed and Breakfast: A guest operation where one to five bedrooms in a residence are 
rented for periods of less than 30 days. The residence containing the bed & breakfast 
operation must be the principal residence of the owner/operator. If meals are provided to 
guests, the owner/operator must comply with the Wyoming Dept. of Agriculture’s food 
handling and permit criteria for bed & breakfast operations. 

2-4 Board: The duly elected Board of County Commissioners of Hot Springs County, 
Wyoming. 

2.5 Campground: A facility for tent camping or recreational vehicle camping, often with 
common facilities such as bathroom, showers, and recreational amenities. All campgrounds 
require land use changes to the Commercial category. 

2-6 Central sewer system: Any sewer system with more than one service connection. 

2-7 Central water system: Public water supply systems and private subdivision water 
supply systems. A public water supply system is a system that has 10 or more service 
connections. A private subdivision water supply system is a system that services three or more 
lots in a subdivision and is not a public water supply. 

2-8 Closed irrigation system: A system for the distribution of irrigation water through a 
system of pipes or other enclosed or covered conduits. 

2-9 Clustering: A method of residential development in which the property owner 
concentrates residences or residential lots on one portion of his/her land in order to leave the 
remainder in agricultural production, wildlife habitat, or open space, or to avoid limitations on 
development such as slopes or floodplains. 

2.10 Commission. The Hot Springs County Land Use Planning Commission.

2-12 Conditions of Approval: Restrictions that may be imposed on the issuance of any

2-13 Condominium: A unit of building or airspace offered for sale, including time sharing. A

2-14 County: Hot Springs County, Wyoming. 

2-15 Day Care Facility (Residential): A State-licensed facility, located within a residence and

2-17 DEQ: The Wyoming Department of Environmental Quality. 

2-18 Development: Any structure, subdivision, mobile home park, or land use change that

2-20. Geothermal: Natural heat, steam, or hot water brought to or near the surface from 
deep geologic formations. 

2-21. Geothermal Energy: Hot water, heat, or electricity generated from the exploitation of 
geothermal resources. 

2-22 Group Home: A State-licensed facility, located within a residence and in a residential 
neighborhood, for the housing and care of handicapped, elderly, or other individuals unable to 
live independently. 

2-23 Historic lands: Lands having pre-historic or historic values, such as battle sites, 
structures and petroglyph sites.

2-24 Home-based Business: Any profession, craft, or other commercial activity located in

2-25 Home occupation: Any profession, craft or other commercial activity conducted in

2-26 Land Use Category: Six land use categories are employed in the unincorporated areas 
of Hot Springs County. These are: 

• Agricultural Land Use Category. Land used primarily for farming or ranching. All 
parcels of land which are not dedicated or used for residential, commercial, 
institutional, or industrial purposes, and which exceed 40 acres in area, are 
considered Agricultural. 
• Residential Land Use Category. Lands designated for residential use, including 
approved residential subdivisions, lands for which a land use change has been 
approved for residential use. Mobile home parks, apartment houses, and other 
multiple family dwellings are allowed with County approval. 
• Commercial Land Use Category. Includes campgrounds, hotels and motels, 
restaurants, retail stores, service stations, and other service industries such as 
professional offices. 
• Industrial Land Use Category. Lands used for industrial purposes such as mining, 
petroleum production and manufacturing. These include industries whose sole 
purpose is the storage, processing and shipping of agricultural products (excepting 
ranches and farms.), timber products, and minerals. Includes wind farms, solar 
arrays, and similar energy-production techniques. 
• Institutional Land Use Category. Churches, schools, hospitals, residential care 
facilities, jails or prisons, and any other public or quasi-public uses. 
• Airport Land Use Category. All lands located within 1,400 linear feet of the County 
Airport runway. Structure heights and certain land uses are regulated in Chapter 
Twelve of this Land Use Plan. 

2-27 Land use change: The conversion of a parcel of land from one land use category to 
another. 

2-28 Legal non-conforming use: A land use that was existing prior to February 7, 1978 that 
is not permitted under this Land Use Plan, which use was not ceased or interrupted for more 
than one year prior to being re-established. 

2-29 Long-term Rentals. A single-family residence, cabin, RV, apartment, or any other type 
of accommodation that is rented for more than 30 days at a time.

2-40 Recreational land: Lands with developed recreational facilities, or lands of high

2-41 Relative policy: A policy which encourages or discourages actions or performance by

CHAPTER THREE 

EXISTING CONDITIONS 

3-1 Agricultural lands. All rural land within Hot Springs County are classified as agricultural 
lands, unless one of the following has taken place: 
• Residential, Commercial, Industrial, or Institutional development has occurred prior 
to February 7, 1978; 
• A Land Use Change has been obtained in accordance with this Land Use Plan or any 
prior Land Use Plan. 

3-2 Areas of Interest. 

A. The Wind River Canyon – From the Wedding of the Waters, south to the Fremont 
County line, extending one-fourth mile back from the top of either rim. Also included 
are the red bluffs or rims, from one-half mile east of the four-mile bridge on Wyoming 
Highway 173, west to a point three miles west of U.S.20, south one and three-quarters 
miles to the Wind River Indian Reservation boundary, then east along said boundary 
four and one-half miles to a point due south of the beginning, thence north to said 
point of beginning. 

1. No structures, other than fences, corrals, livestock watering devices, electrical 
transmission lines, telephone lines and existing access roads shall be built in this 
area on the slopes of Copper and Stagner Mountains. No high profile structures of 
over thirty feet in height shall be constructed along the red rims on the northern 
edge of this scenic area. 

2. There exists, within the canyon, two low-density residential areas and one adjacent 
commercial area. 

B. Hot Springs State Park – Administered by the Parks and Cultural Resources Division of 
the Wyoming Department of Commerce. Agricultural uses should continue on 
contiguous lands east of the river. 

C. Anchor Reservoir – An area located forty miles west of Thermopolis on the south fork of 
Owl Creek, administered by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation. There is a small 
picnic/camp area developed near the inlet of the reservoir. 

D. Big Horn River – This river is the principal feature of Hot Springs County, dividing the 
county from south to north. Along its length are the Wind River Canyon, Wedding of 
the Waters, and Hot Springs State Park. Principal transportation links along this river 
include Highway 20 and the BNSF Railroad. The river is considered to be a Class I and II 

<!-- page: 15 -->

15 
fishing stream, and several Game and Fish easements on either side of the river provide 
fishing and bird hunting access. 
The access points and current uses of the river and its riparian zones for recreational 
purposes should continue. 

E. H Diamond W 4-H Camp – Hot Springs and Washakie Counties have constructed a camp 
for 4-H youth on Grass Creek, on State land near the forest boundary. The camp is also 
leased by other groups for use during the summer. The surrounding area is used for 
grazing. The recreational use of the camp and the agricultural use of the surrounding 
lands should continue. 

F. Shoshone National Forest – Located in the western end of the County, the forest has 
three primary uses: grazing, timber harvest and recreation, especially big game hunting. 
With the acquisition of the road up Grass Creek by the County, it can be expected that 
recreational uses of the forest may increase. 

3-3 Historic Lands. The following site is listed on the National Register of Historic Places: 

A. Legend Rock State Petroglyph Site – This site containing thirty acres on the north bank 
of Cottonwood Creek, is an Indian petroglyph site and an archaeological site. Under 
State ownership, it has been developed by the Parks and Cultural Resources Division of 
the Wyoming Department of Commerce, to accommodate visitors. The current uses of 
adjoining lands visible from and adjacent to the site are agricultural, with Hamilton 
Dome Oil Field immediately across the creek to the south. 

All other historic lands listed below are not enrolled on the National Register of Historic Places, 
but may be eligible for enrollment. 

B. Gebo – The town site in Section 11, T44N, R95W, has largely been obliterated by 
reclamation efforts. Only the remains of a few houses, the water tank, cemetery and 
foundations of some mine buildings and the railroad grade remain. These features 
should be left for future generations to experience. The land use is now agricultural, 
and should continue. 

C. Crosby – Another coal mining town in Section 13, T44N, R95W, in which the town site 
and mine site have largely been obliterated. A layer of coal slack overlays much of the 
area. Surrounding lands are agricultural. Those uses should continue. 

D. Original Townsite – The original site of the Town of Thermopolis, at the mouth of Owl 
Creek, this property is now known as the Longwell Ranch. It still has one of the 
buildings remaining – a saloon – from the original town. An active ranch headquarters 
since the 1890’s, much of this property has now been subdivided. 

<!-- page: 16 -->

16 
The owners should be encouraged to preserve the original Thermopolis building. 
E. Other historic roads – No specific restrictions should be placed on lands crossed by 
these roads. Should construction, mining or other activity take place near the sites of 
these roads, an archaeological survey should be done by an archaeologist approved by 
the State Archaeologist, prior to any surface disturbance. 

1. Buffalo Creek Road – Currently Hot Springs County Road #5, is the original 
Yellowstone Highway between Casper and Thermopolis and dates from 1897 to 
1910. 

2. Mexican Pass Road – This road runs from the Red Canyon Ranch, past Potato Butte 
to Mexican Pass and was the Lander-Thermopolis Stage Road. 

3. Fort Washakie to Red Lodge Stage Road – This road ran from Blonde Pass 
(aka Merritt’s Pass) on Blonde Mountain to the south fork of Owl Creek along 
Twenty-One Creek to Little Grass Creek, to the L U Ranch, then to Enos Creek and 
Gooseberry Creek, then westerly up Gooseberry Creek to Meeteetse. 

4. Indian Trail – This trail crosses the south fork of Owl Creek, eight miles west of 
Blonde Pass in the South Fork Canyon. The trail was used by Indians traveling 
north-northeast across Cottonwood and Gooseberry Creeks. 

5. Bridger Trail – This trail runs over Bridger Saddle and up Bridger Creek to Kirby 
Creek and the Big Horn River, turning north once west of the river. 

F. Ranches of historic interest. 

1. Eagle Nest Ranch – Originally operated as dude ranches by old time area resident 
and movie cowboy, Tim McCoy and his wife Agnes, the current use is primarily 
ranching. The use of these properties should remain in agriculture or in 
recreational use such as dude ranching or outfitting. 

2. Arapahoe Ranch Headquarters – The Arapahoe Ranch, begun in 1884 by Sliney as 
the Padlock Ranch, has several historic buildings at the ranch headquarters. Among 
these are the manager’s house, bookkeeper’s house, bunk house, foreman’s house, 
barn, original commissary and two or three other sheds and barns. Most of the 
structures were built by Rothwell and Simonson during the 1910-25 period. The 
Arapahoe Tribe acquired the property in 1941 and should be encouraged to 
preserve the buildings and adjacent fields in the present use. A commercial use of 
the buildings as a dude complex would not be incompatible with the historic 
character. 

<!-- page: 17 -->

17 
3. L U Ranch – The original L U Ranch headquarters was built on Grass Creek by 
Territorial Governor, George Baxter, about 1884. Three of the original log buildings, 
a house, shed and barn are still standing as of this writing. Also 
used as a stage stop on the Fort Washakie – Red Lodge Stage Road, the agricultural 
setting of this area should be preserved and the owners of the property should be 
encouraged to preserve the original structures. 

4. Dickie Ranch – Currently the headquarters of the L U Ranch at the confluence of 
Enos and Gooseberry Creeks, this headquarters contains historic structures dating 
to its use by the Dickie Brothers and possibly by the original owner, MacDonald. 
The agricultural setting of this headquarters should be retained. 

5. Virgil Rice Ranch – Located west of U.S. Highway 20, on the north side of Owl Creek. 
Homesteaded by Virgil Rice in the 1880’s, the house, though remodeled to some 
extent, is the original house, the oldest occupied house and one of the oldest 
structures in the county. This is still an active farm and should remain in agriculture. 
To the north, west and east lay agricultural lands. 

G. Other Petroglyph Sites – There are some 100 other lesser-known petroglyph sites 
within the county, besides Legend Rock, described above. Care should be taken to not 
deface them during any construction. If digging in near proximity to them were 
proposed, it would be advisable to have an approved archaeologist check the site prior 
to any surface disturbance. 

3-4 Geothermal Energy. The primary available geothermal resource in the County is heat, 
rather than hot water. Current technology includes methods of extracting heat without having 
to extract hot water. The County recognizes that geothermal heat is plentiful, while the 
extraction of hot water – particularly when under artesian pressure – may impact the water 
level and output volume at Big Spring. Consequently, the extraction of geothermal heat is 
encouraged whereas the extraction of geothermal water is discouraged.

CHAPTER FOUR 

PERMITS 

4-1 (This section has been deleted) 

4-2 Permit requirements for Hot Springs County. The following permits are required for 
certain types of development in the County. Each must be processed in accordance with the 
steps described in Section 4-9, except for Subdivisions which are processed in accordance with 
Chapter 5 of this Plan. 

A. Land Use Change Permits: Required for any type of land use change; agricultural to 
residential, industrial or commercial, residential to industrial or commercial, etc. 
Applications are available from the County Planning Office. 

B. Subdivision Permits: The approval of a Preliminary Subdivision Plat constitutes a 
“subdivision permit” as specified in Wyoming Statutes §18-5-302. Subdivision 
applications must comply with the procedural requirements of Chapter 5 
(Subdivisions). Approval of a Preliminary Subdivision Plat shall, if necessary, 
automatically change the Land Use Classification of the property to reflect the 
proposed use. 

C. Residential Business Permit. This permit does not change the Land Use Classification 
of a residential property, but provides for public review of a proposed activity other 
than typical residential use. Such uses include home occupations that exceed the 
criteria in Section 4-5 below, day care facilities, group homes, and bed & breakfast 
facilities. 

D. Gravel Pit Permit. This permit does not change the Land Use Classification of the 
property, and may occur only in the Agricultural Land Use Classification. It 
is a temporary use only, and when it is approved an expiration date must be specified. 

4-3 Legal Non-conforming uses. Any land use existing on February 7, 1978, that is not 
permitted under this Land Use Plan, and not conforming to the standards established herein, is 
a legal non-conforming use and may continue indefinitely subject to the following criteria: 

A. Non-conforming uses cannot be expanded or increased to make them more non-
conforming. They can, however, be changed or amended to make the use less non-
conforming. 

B. If active and continuous use of a legal non-conforming use ceases for a continuous 
period of one year, the non-conforming use shall be considered abandoned and future 

<!-- page: 20 -->

20 
use of the building or parcel of land shall comply with the standards set forth in this 
Land Use Plan. 

C. A legal non-conforming use damaged or destroyed by fire or a similar calamity may be 
restored to its original condition if the restoration is begun within six months and 
completed within one year. Willful destruction of a non-conforming structure or use by 
its owner does not constitute grounds for retaining its non-conforming status. 

4-4 Land use changes. Land use changes are required for all proposed changes in the use 
classification of a given property, including subdivisions, in accordance with W.S.§18-5-203. 
Any non-agricultural construction, earth disturbance, or mining of nonminerals shall require a 
Land Use Change Permit.

CHAPTER SIX 

POLICIES 

6-1 PURPOSE. Prior to taking action on any land use proposal or preliminary subdivision 
plat, the Land Use Planning Commission and the Board of County Commissioners shall 
determine the proposal’s compliance with Absolute Policies and compute its score for Relative 
Policies. Failure to meet any Absolute Policies, or failure to generate a score of zero (0) or 
greater for Relative Policies, shall mandate the denial of the proposal. To limit point losses, the 
applicant may elect to redesign the proposal or propose measures which limit or modify the 
proposed use. 

There are two kinds of policies: 

A. Absolute Policies. These policies require or prohibit certain kinds of performance by the 
applicant. Failure to comply with any absolute policy will result in rejection of an 
application 

B. Relative Policies. These policies encourage or discourage certain kinds of performance 
by developers. Relative policies are weighted on a scale of one through five. A 
development’s performance on each relative policy will be rated on a scale that ranges 
from +2 to –2. An applicant must receive a cumulative score of “0” or better on the 
relative policies to receive approval. 

6-2 ABSOLUTE POLICIES. 

6-2A Agricultural resources policies. These policies are intended to protect the County’s 
agricultural resources and the social and economic activity they support. Agriculture is one of 
the most important sectors of the County’s economy, the most significant use of the County’s 
private and public lands, and an important element to the County’s pastoral landscape. These 
policies deal with the basic agricultural resources – land and water. They also recognize that 
agriculture requires a certain kind of environment in which to operate and prosper. Hot 
Springs County’s productive irrigated croplands are a small and limited portion of its overall 
area, with the majority of the county’s private lands available for development without having 
to impact irrigated lands. 

Farm and ranch operations are vulnerable to many problems when bordered by heavily used 
recreational areas or residential developments. Routine agricultural operations may also be 
perceived as nuisances by recreationists or subdivision residents. Children may be attracted to 
irrigation ditches or to livestock, creating liability problems. Gates may be left open, and dogs 
may prey on livestock. Another concern is the difficulty experienced in allocating irrigation 
water to small parcels, as well as the water management problems created by lot owners 
inexperienced in irrigation procedures. 

<!-- page: 36 -->

36 

A. POLICY A1 – Irrigation Impacts. New developments shall mitigate potential adverse 
impacts on irrigation systems. Developers may be required to fence ditches, to post 
warnings or to re-route roads to reduce the number of ditch crossings. Building 
envelopes may be required on each lot, to locate residences and septic systems away 
from areas of high water table or flood irrigation. 

B. POLICY A2 – Developing Irrigated Lands. If irrigated lands are to be developed, the 
developer shall prepare and institute a water management plan covering the 
assessment of ditch fees or charges, which must be to a single entity (such as a home 
owners’ association) and the internal distribution of water. The water management 
plan must be approved by the appropriate irrigation district or ditch company, or by the 
State Engineer’s Office if no irrigation district or ditch company exists. A copy of the 
approved plan must be submitted to the Commission prior to the final approval of the 
subdivision application. The plan shall include all appropriate applications for change of 
use, change of place of use or change in point of diversion or means of conveyance in 
accordance with W.S.§ 41-3-103, §41-3-104 or §41-3-114. Covenants will be required in 
subdivisions retaining irrigation water. 

C. POLICY A3 – Weed Management Plan Required. The developer and lot owners shall 
control noxious weeds as defined in W.S.§11-5-102, within the rights-of-way of the 
subdivision and upon their private land. A weed management plan shall be prepared. 

6-2B Public services policies. These policies are intended to reduce the public costs of 
private development by providing for adequate improvements in new developments. 

A. Water and Waste Water. 

a. POLICY A4 – Minimum Acreage for Well & Septic. Platted subdivision lots on which 
sewage disposal will be through a small waste water system (septic system) and 
potable water will be provided by an on-site well must be a minimum three (3) 
acres in size to ensure adequate separation of septic tank, leach field, and the well 
head. 

b. POLICY A5 – DEQ Approval for Central Water or Sewer Systems. Central water and 
sewage systems, as well as independent sewage or septic systems, must be 
approved by the Department of Environmental Quality (DEQ), and if located within 
a subdivision, must meet all requirements of W.S. §18-5-306. If no central water or 
sewage system is proposed for the subdivision the final plat and all offers, 
solicitations, advertisements, contracts and agreements relating to that subdivision 
must so indicate. 

<!-- page: 37 -->

37 
c. POLICY A6 – Connecting to a Water or Sewer System. Developments within 1320 
feet (one-quarter mile) of any existing central water or sewage system shall be 
connected to that existing system, unless the system owner certifies that the 
system has insufficient capacity to serve the proposed development. All 
specifications and requirements for connection to the existing system must be met. 

d. POLICY A7 – Package System Maintenance. Developers of industrial, institutional 
and commercial developments, mobile home parks and all subdivisions relying on 
independent central or “package” sewage treatment plants will provide 
documentation of home owners’ association agreements or other binding 
agreements that ensure perpetual maintenance of such systems by qualified 
personnel. 

B. Solid Waste. 

a. POLICY A8 – Solid Waste Removal. Industrial, institutional and commercial 
developments, mobile home park operators and subdivision homeowners’ 
associations shall provide for weekly solid waste haulage, in a covered vehicle or 
trailer, to an approved landfill or transfer station. Solid waste collection points shall 
be designated in the development’s site plan and shall be screened or protected to 
prevent blowing trash and other nuisance problems. 

C. Access. 

a. POLICY A9 – Highway Access Permits. Highway accesses must be developed 
pursuant to a permit issued by the Wyoming Department of Transportation. Access 
points on public highways shall be located and spaced to meet or exceed the 
minimum sight distances required by the Wyoming Department of Transportation. 

b. POLICY A10 – Access Right-of-way. If the developer proposes to utilize adjoining 
property for roadway access to the subdivision, a minimum access roadway right-of-
way or easement of sixty (60) feet to the development shall be provided. 

c. POLICY A11 – Subdivision Utilities. Adequate access for and service capability by 
private utilities shall be certified by each utility involved. 

d. POLICY A12 – Subdivision Roads. Residential developments shall provide all 
weather public access roads capable of servicing emergency response 
vehicles to all lots or units. Adequate rights-of-way for all such roads shall be 
indicated on the subdivision plat.

e. POLICY A13 – Utility Access to Development. If the subdivider proposes to utilize 
adjoining property for sewers, drainage, sewer lines power lines or other utilities, 
the subdivider shall provide copies of binding easements of not less than twenty 
(20) feet in width for the proposed facilities from each property owner over whose

D. Roads. 

a. POLICY A14 – Road Construction Standards. All access roads to Federal, State or

b. POLICY A15 – Internal Road Maintenance. Commercial, institutional and industrial

CHAPTER ELEVEN 

VARIANCE PROCEDURES 

11-1 Definition. A variance is an administrative authorization for property to be used 
in a manner departing from the literal requirements of this Land Use Plan and/or 
Wyoming State Statutes. 

11-2 Purposes and limitations. In order to prevent or reduce practical difficulties 
and unnecessary physical hardships that are inconsistent with the purposes of this Land 
Use Plan, resulting from its strict or literal interpretation, the Board may grant 
variances. The procedure is intended to resolve practical difficulties or unnecessary 
physical hardships, which may result from the size, shape or dimensions of a site or the 
location of existing structures thereon; from topographic or physical conditions on the 
site or in the immediate vicinity; or from physical limitations, road locations or traffic 
conditions, in the immediate vicinity. Cost or inconvenience to the applicant may be 
given consideration, but shall not be the sole reason for granting a variance. 

11-3 Standards. Before recommending or granting a variance, the Commission and 
Board shall make the following findings: 

A. That there are special circumstances which are peculiar to the land or building 
for which the variance is sought, and which do not apply generally to land or 
buildings in the neighborhood or that are similarly situated. 

B. That those circumstances or conditions are such that the strict application of the 
provisions of the Land Use Plan would deprive the applicant of the reasonable 
use of the land or building, and that the variance, if granted, is the minimum 
adjustment that will accomplish the purpose. 

C. That the granting of the variance is in harmony with the general purposes and 
intent of the Land Use Plan and will not be injurious to the neighborhood or 
otherwise detrimental to the public welfare. 

D. That the granting of the variance will not constitute a grant of special privilege 
inconsistent with the limitations on similarly impaired properties in the County. 

E. That the variance will not resolve a hardship created by the conduct or activity 
of the owner or a prior owner. 

11-4 Application procedures. 

<!-- page: 60 -->

60 

A. Variance applications shall be filed by the owner of the property, in the County 
Planning Office, on the prescribed form. The County application form shall be 
accompanied by the following: 

1. A letter stating the precise nature of the variance requested, the regulation 
involved and the practical difficulty or unnecessary physical hardship that 
would result from strict or literal interpretation and enforcement of the 
specified regulation. 

2. For dimensional variances, a site plan drawn to scale, including a degree of 
detail appropriate to the complexity of the requested variance, showing all 
existing and proposed features on the site and on adjoining sites, if 
necessary, pertinent to the requested variance. 

3. Such additional material as may be pertinent to the application and the 
findings prerequisite to the granting of a variance. 

B. Upon submission of the completed application to the County Planning Office, 
the planning staff shall process the application in accordance with the Standard 
Review Process outlined in Section 4-9 of this Plan. 

11-5 Abandonment or Lapse. A variance shall lapse and become null and 
void one year following the date on which the variance was granted unless, 
prior to the expiration date, construction or development has commenced. 
However, the Board may extend authorization for an additional six months upon 
written request. 

11-6 Suspension and Revocation. A variance granted subject to a condition 
or conditions shall be suspended automatically upon failure to comply with one 
or more conditions. If not satisfied that the condition is being met, the Board 
may revoke the variance or take such actions as it deems necessary, to ensure 
compliance with the condition. 

11-7 Re-application. Following denial or revocation of a variance, no 
application for a variance for the same or site shall be filed unless it is 
determined that there is new and substantial information to support the re-
application. 

11-8 Effects of future sale. The approved variance shall remain with the land 
for so long as the property continues to be used for the same purpose.

CHAPTER TWELVE 

COUNTY AIRPORT AREA RESTRICTIONS 

12.1 Purpose. In order to ensure the safety of aircraft using the Hot Springs County 
Airport, and also to prevent the introduction of structures and activities which may be 
threatened by aircraft activity, this Chapter establishes the following restrictions: 

1. Limits the height of structures and other obstacles within certain distances 
from the airport runway; 
2. Prohibits placing certain activities involving public assembly within airport 
approach areas; 
3. Recognizes the County’s right and obligation to remove any obstacle that 
protrudes into the operating airspace of the airport and its approach zones. 

12.2 Airport Area Established. The County Airport Area is hereby established, 
containing all public and private lands within 1,400 linear feet of the centerline of the 
airport runway, and within one mile of e0ach end of the runway. All lands within this 
area shall comply with the regulations specified in this chapter, in addition to any 
limitations specified for the appropriate Land Use Category for those lands. This chapter 
shall supplement the Land Use Category of the property, and is not itself a stand-alone 
Land Use Category. 

12.3 Airport Area Map Established. Using appropriate FAA maps, guidelines, and 
regulations, the County shall prepare and maintain a map of the Airport Area for the Hot 
Springs County Airport. This map shall be available to the public in the County Planning 
Office, and shall specify distances and height restrictions for all lands within the Airport 
Area. 

12.4 Height Limits. All existing and future construction within the Airport Area shall 
comply with the maximum heights allowed by the Airport Area Map. Failure to comply 
with the height limit may require lighting and/or engineering in accordance with FAA 
guidelines. Modification or removal of structures may also be mandated. 

12.5 Land Use Restrictions. Within the area defined in Section 12.2 above, certain 
land uses or activities which may concentrate large groups of people shall be prohibited. 
These may include, but are not limited to, schools, theatres, churches, shopping centers, 
and other places of assembly. 

12.6 Removal of Obstructions. Hot Springs County reserves the right to remove or 
reduce any obstruction that protrudes into the protected airspace described in Section

CHAPTER THIRTEEN 

GEOTHERMAL HEAT 

13-1 Geothermal Permit Required. All methods of accessing or utilizing geothermal 
heat in Hot Springs County must first obtain a Geothermal Permit from the County 
Planning Office. The purpose of the permit is to ensure that the geothermal aquifer is 
protected through the use of heat exchangers. Applicants are advised that permits 
must also be obtained from the Wyoming State Engineer’s Office for the use of any 
surface water or groundwater, including geothermal water. 

13-2 Extraction of Geothermal Water Prohibited. The use of geothermal water for 
any purpose including – but not limited to – heated water, building heat, recreation, 
livestock water, and irrigation is prohibited. The extraction and dumping of geothermal 
water is also prohibited. 

13-3 Pre-existing Uses Exempt. Existing wells, artesian wells, ditches, and other 
conveyances of geothermal water are exempt from the prohibitions and permit 
requirements of this Chapter. Pre-existing uses of geothermal heat extraction are also 
exempt from the requirements of this Chapter. This Chapter shall become effective on 
the date of adoption of this Land Use Plan, and is not retroactive. 

13-4 Penalties. Failure to comply with the requirements of this Chapter may result in 
prosecution as a misdemeanor offense. Each day of violation shall constitute a new 
offense. 

13-4 Variance. Applicants claiming to have unique or extraordinary circumstances 
which prevent compliance with the requirements of this Chapter may apply for a 
variance in accordance with Chapter Eleven of this Land Use Plan.
