# TX Permian Basin & Panhandle — coverage notes (2026-09-04)

## The county-permit reality: no zoning, but four real levers

Texas counties have no general zoning authority (this is a state-constitutional/statutory limit,
not a policy choice), so nothing in this file records a county "zoning permit" for oil & gas
surface facilities. What counties in the Permian and Panhandle actually administer, and what a
midstream project should expect to touch, is narrower but real:

1. **Subdivision platting** — Local Government Code Ch. 232 gives counties authority to require
   plat approval for any division of land outside a city's ETJ. Midland, Ector, Howard, and Gaines
   all publish subdivision regulations that fold in road-construction standards and floodplain
   compliance statements. A midstream project that requires carving out or replatting a surface
   tract (compressor station pad, SWD site, tank battery) for lease/easement purposes can trigger
   this even though the county has no zoning to enforce against the use itself.
2. **County road ROW / utility line installation & road-use agreements** — every county in this
   file administers this in some form through its Road & Bridge (or "Public Works"/"Engineering
   Services") department: utility and pipeline crossings of county roads, and — critically in the
   Permian — road-use/road-repair agreements for the heavy trucking associated with drilling and
   midstream construction. Reeves County's dedicated "Permits and Regulations" page and Permit
   Inspector role, and Ector County's Engineering Services review, are the most concretely
   documented examples found.
3. **Floodplain administration (NFIP)** — every county participates in some form of floodplain
   management, usually folded into the subdivision regulations (Ector, Midland, Gaines) or run
   as a discrete process (Pecos County's FFRMS public-notice process for county-adjacent
   floodplain activity near Fort Stockton).
4. **911 addressing and OSSF permits** — largely unverified at the site level in this pass (see
   "Not verified" below); Gaines County's regulations document explicitly reference on-site
   sewage facility (OSSF) standards, which is the one concrete confirmation found. Assume every
   county in this file runs an OSSF program per state law (30 TAC Ch. 285) even where not directly
   confirmed — man-camps, field offices, and remote compressor stations on septic systems will
   need this.

A useful way to frame a county record in this registry: "no zoning, but does platting +
road/ROW + floodplain + OSSF/911" is the default assumption for every unlisted-detail county
below; only the top-tier counties (Midland, Ector, Reeves, Howard, Gaines, Pecos) have that
assumption backed by an actual document citation.

## Cities: which ones have real post-HB 40 oil & gas ordinances

HB 40 (2015, Tex. Nat. Res. Code §81.0523) gives the state (via the Railroad Commission)
exclusive jurisdiction over oil & gas operations and limits municipalities to "commercially
reasonable" regulation of aboveground activity — fire/emergency response, traffic, notice,
setbacks, noise, lighting, reclamation. Confirmed, currently-codified ordinances with real
teeth found in this pass:

- **City of Midland** — Oil Code (City Code Ch. 6-1, Sec. 6-1-23) governing wells permitted
  in-city since 2010, plus a separate Article VIII Oil and Gas Pipeline Standards (Ch. 34) that
  is the most directly midstream-relevant municipal provision found anywhere in this file.
- **City of Odessa** — Article 4-14 Oil and Gas Drilling, requiring a permit for any well,
  trunkline pipeline, waterflood, or injection/recovery system; the ordinance text still invokes
  "reasonable and prudent operator" practice standards that likely need an HB 40 reasonableness
  read before being treated as enforceable beyond surface impact.
- **City of Andrews** — Article V Oil and Gas Wells (Ord. 1575, adopted the same year as HB 40),
  a genuinely active, insurance-and-permit-driven ordinance with a 12-month retroactive
  compliance window and an industrial-zoning grandfather clause.
- **City of Denver City** — Article 4.04 Oil and Gas Drilling, requiring a city permit for any
  drilling/workover/recompletion on a producing or injection well within city limits, cross-
  referencing RRC rules for populated-area operations.

Cities where a code platform was located but **no dedicated oil & gas chapter was confirmed**
(treat as either genuinely absent or simply not surfaced by search — do not assume HB 40
preemption has left nothing to check): Big Spring, Monahans, Kermit, Pecos City, Fort Stockton,
Seminole, Alpine, Borger, Pampa, Canadian, Dumas, Stinnett, Spearman. City of Amarillo has
confirmed fire/safety provisions for gas utility stations and installations (Title XVIII, Art.
III) and a fugitive-gas-control chapter (Ch. 4-3, Art. V) — both are the kind of "commercially
reasonable" surface/fire-code regulation HB 40 leaves standing, and both are directly relevant
to a compressor station or metering facility sited inside city limits.

**Do not record, anywhere in this file, a city permit that would require RRC-jurisdiction
subsurface approval, spacing, or technical operating conditions** — HB 40 preempts those; only
the surface/fire/traffic/notice layer belongs here.

## H2S / SWR 36 relevance in the Delaware Basin sour counties

Not independently re-verified this pass (general regulatory-domain knowledge, flagged here
rather than left silent): Railroad Commission Statewide Rule 36 (16 TAC §3.36) is the H2S
public-safety rule — contingency planning, public-area radius calculations, warning signage,
and RRC Form H-9/H-15 filings — that applies wherever a well or associated gathering/gas
facility is classified as a "Hydrogen Sulfide Area." The Delaware Basin counties in this
registry with the strongest known H2S/sour-gas reputation are **Reeves, Culberson, Loving,
Ward, Pecos, and Winkler** — this drove the priority-tier assignment for those counties, but
none of the county-level records in this file cite an actual SWR 36 H2S Area determination or
RRC contingency-plan filing for a specific facility; that lookup is facility-specific (RRC
GIS/H-9 filings) and out of scope for a county-landing-page pass. Any Form 1.9 review touching
these six counties should independently pull the RRC H2S Public Safety GIS layer and confirm
whether the specific route/site falls inside a designated H2S Area before relying on this file
alone.

  Permian cities, search did not surface a specific oil & gas / pipeline ordinance chapter. That
  is not proof the ordinance doesn't exist — eCode360/Municode full-text search often requires
  navigating the table of contents rather than a web search — it means this pass found the code
  platform and landing page but not the specific chapter, and confidence is set to `unverified`
  accordingly.
- **GIS layers**: no county- or city-level ArcGIS REST endpoint was found and confirmed live for
  any Permian or Panhandle jurisdiction in this pass; the `gis` array is empty in every record in
  this file. This is the single biggest gap relative to the CO source file's depth and should be
  the first thing a follow-up pass targets, starting with Midland, Ector, and Reeves counties'
  Public Works/Engineering GIS portals.
- **Contact directories** (direct phone/email for a named floodplain administrator or road permit
  official) were confirmed for only Midland, Ector, Reeves, Loving, Howard, and Reagan counties.
